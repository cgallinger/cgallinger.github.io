To wander off my celestial path and come back down to our own blue marble for a bit, today I wanted to talk about a really nice paper I recently read that provides even more evidence for the biospheric crisis humanity is causing with our ongoing addiction to fossil fuels. Longtime readers will recall the [last time](https://cgallinger.github.io/blog/the-missing-metric-of-the-climate-crisis/) I dipped my toe into the realm of climate science, focusing on what anthropogenic climate change denialists frequently get wrong (or deliberately obfuscate) about why climate change is a problem, and today we're taking on another of their favourite arguments: [_"Plants need CO2 to grow, and CO2 levels are near the lowest they've been in our planet's history, so it's actually a **good** thing we're adding more to the atmosphere!"_](https://web.archive.org/web/20220820002034/https://moxiyo.com/co2-green-c02-carbon-emissions-water-gas/)

While it's true that current CO2 levels in the Earth's atmosphere are much lower than they have been for most of its geologic history, there are several reasons why this argument is fallacious. First of all, as mentioned in my last climate science post, the problem with our current atmospheric trajectory is the _rate of change_, _**not**_ the absolute magnitude of global average temperature or greenhouse gas concentration. 

Specifically in relation to food crops (often used as a key lynchpin in the argument by CO2 boosters, who claim we're actually helping alleviate world hunger with our ravenous fossil fuel consumption), the issues of heat stress and increased droughts will actually lead to significantly _declines_ in the overall output of most grains (aside from wheat, which may see short-term mild increases in production before dropping if warming continues past the end of the century). Alongside carbon dioxide, another major chemical humans are adding in massive quantities to the Earth system is nitrogen, primarily in the form of nitrates used for fertilizers, which have allowed for the expansion in industrial agriculture that has sustained the [major global population growth](https://ourworldindata.org/how-many-people-does-synthetic-fertilizer-feed) and [decrease in world hunger](https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/rising-yields-falling-hunger) seen throughout the 20th century. Unfortunately, while an important nutrient for crops, excess nitrates delivered to the environment via runoff have led to [considerable problems with eutrophication](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13280-020-01466-x)<span class="ref"><span class="refnum">[1]</span><span class="refbody">the process of deoxygenation in aquatic environments due to excess growth followed by death and decomposition of nutrient-limited microorganisms, usually seen in the form of "algae blooms" (predominantly cyanobacteria)</span></span>,
 and by some estimates the global introduction of anthropogenic nitrates has [exceeded safe thresholds for planetary biochemical stability](https://www.stockholmresilience.org/research/planetary-boundaries.html) by as much as 2x. This would seem to suggest that, on average, plants primarily have far more nitrogen available than they need, and thus (ignoring the aforementioned issues with heat stress) increasing CO2 would only lead to increased plant mass. Right?

It turns out that whether ecosystems such as forests located away from sites of high anthropogenic N input are generally in a state of eutrophication (increasing/excess N) or oligotrophication (declining/deficient N) has actually been a matter of considerable debate in the past several decades, and the answer has major implications for future climate projections, as [boreal forests in Canada alone account for ~12% of the planet's land-based carbon reserves](https://www.borealconservation.org/giant-carbon-shield).
