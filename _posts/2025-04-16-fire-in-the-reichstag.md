It's only been four months, and yet already 2025 has started to feel like a surreal production.

Last year at this time, we were in the middle of a strike for a better contract with the university, a fight which ultimately ended . Shortly after, my life would be radically changed by the start of the [Gaza solidarity encampment at Western](https://cgallinger.github.io/blog/14-days/), and the subsequent summer and fall of protests, actions, and campaigns to stop the funding and arming of the genocidal Israeli regime. It was a year that, for me, blossomed with solidarity for struggles across the globe, from Palestine to Congo, Ukraine to Sudan, Bangladesh to Syria. It was the first time since moving to London that I felt actually connected to people both in my community and around the globe, something I'd dearly missed from my life in Toronto.

But oh, how I did not expect the vultures of fascism to roost so uncomfortably close to home.

## A Tremor in the Earth

It's now been a little over 12 weeks since Trump's inauguration, and I've watched many in my life observe the subsequent institutional chaos and destruction with a relatively detached level of concern, seeing it as more melodrama from the ever-hyperbolic American political theatre. In many ways I, too, have had to find a way to keep moving through life as though the seemingly-hourly seismic shifts in the norms of democracy had no real bearing on me, simply in order to keep progressing in my work. But at the same time, I've been keeping an eye on international events with increasing concern, and this week they finally directly intersected with me:

Yesterday, I informed my advisor that I would be backing out from a planned lab trip to the US southwest for a science team meeting and field work study.

Of course, this was not a decision made entirely out of the blue. On March 21, I was [interviewed by a reporter from _Nature_](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-00859-w) about the shockwaves sent through the scientific community by a researcher from France being detained and having his belongings confiscated before being turned back by US border security. The story was even more impactful as, judging by the details about the researcher, he was likely going to the [Lunar and Planetary Science Conference](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2025/), the largest annual conference in our field.

Now, personally, it's been years since I've even thought about attending LPSC in person. My last intended visit was going to be in 2020, where I was scheduled to give my first-ever talk at a conference (as opposed to only presenting in the poster session). Of course, given that the conference is held in mid-March, you can guess how that year worked out: COVID immediately wiped those plans off the map, and then communication errors meant I didn't even give my virtual "lightning talk" at the rescheduled time in April.

In 2021, I returned to academia to start my PhD, but in the four years since the political conditions and climate in the location where the conference is held---The Woodlands, a weird concrete-savannah planned community in Texas---made the thought of returning feel increasingly unsafe. Texas in particular has been one of the most vociferous states in proposing or passing anti-trans legislation, [leading the pack]() on the current wave that has [swept the United States over the past five years](https://translegislation.com/learn). In March, a bill was introduced that would make even [the very act of identifying as trans a jailable offense](https://www.nbcnews.com/nbc-out/out-politics-and-policy/texas-bill-identify-transgender-state-felony-rcna195642).

Of course, all of this was still somewhat hypothetical and more out of principle than genuine direct threat to my safety---the majority of these bills are aimed at policing and controlling trans children, and not at random adults passing through for a week. But this year, all of that changed.

## (Il)liberal Hauntings

Although it never made into the final piece, during that interview back in March I went on at length to the reporter about how none of this is really new. For two decades since its establishment, we've witnessed the US's ICE agency [disappear and deport thousands of migrants]() merely for seeking a better life in the so-called land of the free. We've experienced [escalating police brutality](), both at home and south of the border, ultimately leading to the [Black Lives Matter]() movement after the murder of Michael Brown in 2013, and [re-invigorating it with extraordinary intensity]() following the killing of George Floyd in 2020. We've seen [homophobia and transphobia insidiously multiplying in waves](https://xtramagazine.com/power/activism/anti-trans-violence-2024-269144) that begat some of the [worst mass attacks on our community](https://www.them.us/story/two-years-after-pulse) in the history of Canada and the US. And, of course, we've witnessed an [unparalleled rise in xenophobia and anti-immigrant racism]() that far preceded the results of the most recent American election, with [similarly horrific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_City_mosque_shooting) and [deadly consequences](https://theconversation.com/muslim-family-killed-in-terror-attack-in-london-ontario-islamophobic-violence-surfaces-once-again-in-canada-162400).

From this perspective, my newfound fear could perhaps be seen as an over-reaction---"_why weren't you this concerned all those years before?_" The thing is, I _have_ been concerned, and as mentioned above, made conscious choices when I felt the level of danger I'd experience or legitimacy I'd lend to certain vindictive political entities would outweigh the benefit of any potential travel within their borders. I've also stood in solidarity with my siblings across all of these struggles; never for a second have I naively assumed these were new problems, brought about purely because Orange Man Bad. No, the difference now is one of scope and scale---about a palpable, personal threat to my safety that until now I'd assumed I was too privileged to really be affected by.

## The American _Schutzstaffel_

Of course, it's hard to overstate how much of this personal trepidation is due to the newly-normalized policies of persecution over political stances in the US. For everything I criticize about its oppressive, hyper-capitalist, neo-colonial existence, until recently the one thing the US really did have going for it was its vociferous, almost monomaniacal commitment to "free speech", that oh-so-darling cause of the post-Gamergate clean-your-room-bucko alt-right. As much as I do think certain harmful speech should be actively shunned and countered by society, the enshrining of its protection in the US Constitution did mean I had less to fear of direct reprisal for being open about my beliefs---it was certainly something I never really thought about when crossing the border previously, despite my decade+ presence on social media openly opposing the oppressive actions of both the Canadian and US states.

But now?

For me, the [arrest and disappearing of Mahmoud Khalil]() on March 8 was a watershed moment in what the next four years would look like. While supporters of Palestine, especially those of us more active in anti-genocide protests of the past year and a half, have [long been wary of reprisals](https://breachmedia.ca/inside-the-shocking-police-operations-targeting-pro-palestine-activists-in-toronto/) by our institutions and the state,<span class="ref"><span class="refnum">[1]</span><span class="refbody">hence why so many of us show up masked or "blocked up" to protect our anonymity during demonstrations, despite the [demonization of such safety practices by bad-faith critics]()</span></span> for the most part we were only truly vulnerable during the occasional more aggressive actions we took to push back against our silencing. Outside of direct physical police confrontation, we knew our personal speech was unassailable as a legal and civil matter (leaving aside our confidence in its moral validity and integrity). But when Mr. Khalil was kidnapped from his own apartment on that evening in March, all of that changed.


## The Home Front

Of course, regardless of how things play out in the US, we have our own, perhaps more pressing version of these political shifts playing out at home. In just 12 days, we will be taking to the polls here to choose our next federal government, and the outcome of that election may set the stage for how closely we follow the US playbook over the next four years. Pierre Poilievre, who until Trudeau resigned and Trump was inaugurated seemed [essentially guaranteed to win](), has spent two years rallying his base around talking points that [eerily echo those on the Republican campaign trail](), much as he may now be desperately backpedaling in a bid to distance himself from the massive unpopularity of their real-life consequences. ## Future, Tense

Truthfully, it's been a difficult time to stay focused on my esoteric extra-terrestrial research the past few months while all of this has been transpiring. On top of these immediate and direct threats, the acceleration of climate collapse has been weighing on my mind more than ever, and as I approach the end of this PhD journey I can't help but be caught up in stewing over what I'll actually be able to do to help mitigate it with my accumulated planetological knowledge.

As I finish this post, I discovered that yesterday [an advisory was sent out by the Canadian Association of University Teachers](https://www.caut.ca/latest/2025/04/caut-advises-academics-against-non-essential-travel-us) urging cessation of non-essential travel to the U.S., specifically calling out, among others "[t]hose who have expressed negative opinions about the current U.S. administration or its policies" and "[t]ravellers who identify as transgender or whose travel documents indicate a sex other than their sex assigned at birth".
