A couple weeks ago our lab group finally watched _Contact_ together for the first time, the 1997 film based on the 1985 novel by Carl Sagan. For both our lab supervisor and I, it was one of the most formative pieces of media in our youths, and frankly I was baffled that no one else in our lab had seen it (at least in its entirety) before. I was four years old when it originally was released and so didn't see it until it made the TV rerun circuit several years later, but from that first viewing it stuck with me like no other film since. I've [talked before](https://cgallinger.github.io/blog/adios-arecibo/) about its significance to my scientific journey as well as its importance for representation of women in STEM, but there's another aspect that has persistently returned like a tide in my mind, yet I rarely see discussed amongst its scientific stans: the dialogue between science and religion.

This theme lies at the heart of both the film and the book, and indeed was a subject that Sagan returned to many times throughout his work. There is even a collection of the Gifford Lectures he delivered in 1985---the same year he published _Contact_---that were edited and published posthumously by his wife, Ann Druyan, as _The Varieties of Scietific Experience_, in direct reference to the compendium of lectures on natural theology delivered under the same auspices by Williams James in 1901-1902. Perhaps most famously, his enduringly popular book and TV series, _Cosmos_, also explored the awe of the universe with the same reverence with which the ancients beheld the awe of the divine.


Yet along with this almost spiritual view on the relationship between humans and the Cosmos, there was a darker undercurrent present throughout Sagan's writing: his fear and repulsion at the (perceived) anti-intellectualism and irrationality of religion. You may have seen this quote shared around on the internet from Sagan's final book _The Demon-Haunted World_:

<a href="{{ imgpath }}/demon_haunted_world.jpg">![image-center]({{ imgpath }}/demon_haunted_world.jpg){: .align-center}</a>

To many, this passage from 1995 was eerily prescient about the polarized, post-Trump, algorithmically-manipulated society we've been inhabiting since the mid-2010s, and to a large extent I agree with these assessments. But Sagan's use of language here is, I think, revealing: that the form in which anti-intellectualism manifests is through "clutching crystals" and "nervously consulting horoscopes". Certainly, there are pseudoscientific new-age practitioners today who participate in upholding damaging and dangerous beliefs. But by and large, it feels like astrologists, as in times past, have not been the predominant purveyors of fascist ideology. As in the late 19th-early 20th century, today we see many of the biggest champions of "rationalism" leading the vanguard of bigotry and anti-humanism.

I think it is here where the limits of Sagan's understanding of history and religion really begin to affect the soundness of his writing. [This blog post](https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2020/02/05/carl-sagan-was-really-bad-at-history/) has a really excellent breakdown of the many historical inaccuracies found throughout Sagan's _Cosmos_---most infamously, his complete misrepresentation of the "destruction" of the Library of Alexandria and the martyrdom of the philosopher Hypatia, implicating the Christianization of Rome in both of these acts and the abandonment of its great philosophical tradition. In fact, Sagan does not just propagate these myths: he may have been _responsible_ for many of them coming into popular awareness in the first place.

The anti-religious prejudices and inaccuracies in _Contact_ are more subtle, but I think significantly compromise what is otherwise an exceptional exploration of the relationship between science and spirituality. Leaving aside the accuracy of the statistic (and how the demographics of religiosity are even researched/quantified), the framing of the preponderance of world religions as believing in a "Supreme Being" belies Sagan's unfortunately Christo-centric/Abrahamic bias. A favourite target of many New Atheists, the idea of religions revolving around some kind of "sky daddy" is in fact an extremely intellectually bereft one, and betrays an incuriosity jarringly out of step with the depth and complexity with which Sagan otherwise treats his subjects. In fact, even many of the most celebrated figures in rationalist history and philosophy, from Aristotle to Francis Bacon to Isaac Newton, held deeply spiritual and religious beliefs, and in their time saw no contradiction between these and the methodological technologies they were innovating that we would eventually come to call science.

## The New Gods

There is a perverse irony in the fact that many of the people<span class="ref"><span class="refnum">[1]</span><span class="refbody">Let's be real here: men</span></span> inspired by the cosmic visions of Sagan in their youth are now directly responsible for the current wave of technofascist dystopia we are slowly being consumed by. The fact that someone like Elon Musk, the richest man in the world, can both cite Sagan and Asimov and other progressive scientific thinkers as inspiration for his interplanetary ambitions, while simultaneously championing paranoid Great Replacement conspiracies and xenophobic

Musk has even directly [read passages](https://twitter.com/JohnCarlos/status/1361093240280129541?s=20&mx=2) from _Pale Blue Dot_ in interviews, citing Sagan among other giants of 20th century science and science fiction as inspiration for his "multi-planetary" vision of humanity. 


## No Heroes

In truth, it is perhaps only because Sagan passed away when he did---post-fall of the Soviet Union, pre-9/11---that he is remembered so fondly among liberals and progressives today. There is little doubt in my mind that the undercurrents of disdain for (organized) religion present throughout his work would have manifested in the same virulent Islamophobia subsequently championed by the likes of Dawkins, Hitchens, and co. Indeed, it's this aspect of his work that makes me the most uncomfortable to revisit today, and it is also where I see the seed of modern "rationalist" white supremacy, transphobia, and xenophobia.
